Here We Are

Our college spends more each year than it brings in, and doing a little less of the same thing will not fix that. This section explains why in plain terms, and why the answer depends on all of us.

Fewer students, more choices

The number of Americans finishing high school peaked in 2025 at roughly 3.8 to 3.9 million. It is projected to fall about 13 percent by 2041, and 38 states will have fewer graduates in 2041 than in 2023. The Northeast and Midwest have already started to decline.

Fewer students means every college competes harder for each one. The most common response is a bigger scholarship. Private colleges now give back an average of about 57 percent of sticker tuition to first-year students as their own aid, a record. Each point of discount is money the college never collects.

The core question

Even after the decline, more than three million people will finish high school every year. Millions of adults want a degree or a new skill. They will all go somewhere. How do we make sure it is here?

What the market wants decides what we can sell

Think of a concert hall with empty seats. If people do not want to hear the program, a bigger poster will not fill the hall. Better marketing tells more people what we offer. It cannot make them want it.

Business people call this product/market fit: offering something enough people want, at a price they will pay, that costs less to deliver than it brings in. When a college has a fit problem, the offering has to change: what we teach, how, when, where, for whom and at what price.

How big is the market? TAM, SAM and SOM

Before anyone argues for an idea, we should know how many people might want it. Investors size a market in three steps, and the same steps work for a college.

TermWhat it means for a college
TAM: Total Addressable MarketEveryone who could want this kind of education and whom we could plausibly reach. For example, adults in our region who started college and never finished.
SAM: Serviceable Available MarketThe part of the TAM we can actually serve: close enough to campus or within our online reach, interested in fields we teach, ready for our academic level and able to pay our net price.
SOM: Serviceable Obtainable MarketThe share of the SAM we can realistically win each year within three years, after the community college, the state university and the big online programs take their share.

The TAM always looks large and means little. The SOM is the number that pays salaries. Be wary of any SOM above a few percent of the SAM unless the interviews in Phase 2 back it up, because every competitor is reading the same demographic charts we are.

What the example teaches

A good idea with real demand closed about a tenth of the gap. Most colleges will need two or three ideas, a smaller cost base, or both. The board should expect that answer and plan for it from the start.

Every college needs its own answer

There is no template for a rescued college. What works for a nursing college in a small city will not work for an arts college in the mountains or a faith-based college in the suburbs. The plan that saves us will build on what is already true about us: our place, our people, our reputation and the things students say we do better than anyone nearby.

The tools in this workbook are the same for every college. The answer will be ours, and it will come from the evidence we gather in Phase 2 and from an honest look at who we are.

Why the people closest to the college should not set the direction

Current students chose us. Faculty built what we offer. Both are the people for whom the current college already works. That makes them the right people to tell us what must not be lost, and the wrong people to tell us what the students who are not choosing us want.

This workbook asks faculty and students to do real work: supply facts, propose ideas, test them, design the new offering and deliver it. The direction itself comes from evidence about the students we are trying to reach.

Why we are sharing more than usual

Colleges in trouble often keep the numbers quiet, hope next year's class is bigger and protect the identity they have. Each of those feels prudent. Together they use up the time and money needed to change.

Plans also fail when leaders decide in a small room, agree too quickly and avoid being wrong in public. The cure is more people looking at the same facts, and someone whose job is to say the plan will not work.

The Six Ideas That Explain Every College Budget

You do not need a business degree to use them, and you do need all six to judge any idea for the college's future.

1. Sticker Price & Net Price

The sticker price is the published tuition. Almost no one pays it. The net price is what a student actually pays after the college's own scholarships and grants.

2. Discount Rate

The share of sticker tuition the college gives back as its own aid. If tuition is $50,000 and the average student receives $27,500 in college-funded aid, the discount rate is 55%.

3. Net Tuition Revenue

Students times net price. This is the money that pays salaries. A college can enroll more students and still collect less money if it discounts more to get them.

4. Cost Per Student

Total operating spending divided by students. Most of that cost is fixed in the short run: buildings, debt, most salaries.

5. Contribution

What one more student or program brings in minus the costs that go away if it goes away. A program with negative contribution makes the gap bigger every year it runs.

6. Cash Runway

How many months the college can pay its bills from cash and investments it is allowed to spend, at its current rate of loss. Runway is the clock.

Key lesson

Discounting more to fill seats can make the gap worse. Raise the discount rate from 55% to 60% and each student brings in $2,500 less. Across 1,500 students that is $3.75 million a year.

Three Rules for the Whole Year

  1. Numbers first, opinions second. Every idea gets tested against the same worksheets.
  2. The people who are here help build the future, and the people who are not here tell us what it must be. Prospective students set the direction.
  3. Change moves at the speed of trust. Share more than feels comfortable, on a schedule, and never share a number you will have to take back.

Red Team Checklist

  • Three to five people, named by the board
  • At least one with finance expertise from outside the college
  • At least one from outside higher education
  • No one who wrote the plan under review
  • Access to the same data as the cabinet
Habits that keep a small room honest

The president speaks last in every decision meeting. Run a premortem: before approving a plan, everyone writes privately why it failed two years from now. Write forecasts down with dates and authors. Reward the bad news.

Interview Guide

  1. Tell me how you decided where to go. What were the finalists?
  2. What did the place you chose offer that the others did not?
  3. What did you think of us? Where did that impression come from?

CABINET FACULTY STUDENTS

Phase 3: Design and Test Ideas

By day 120, working teams turn the strongest ideas into one-page A3 proposals. Each A3 goes through at least three rounds of hard questions, gets priced by the CFO and faces the red team before the board sees it. Anyone can submit an idea.

Step 1: The Idea Card

One idea per card. Keep it to one page. To see what a market read looks like, study the Market Examples tab, then do your own.

Step 2: The Screen

An idea moves to an A3 team only if the answer to all six is yes.

  • We have evidence of demand from people who are not current students
  • At full size it closes a meaningful percent of the annual gap within three years
  • We can test it with real applications and deposits within twelve months
  • We have, or can affordably get, the people and space to deliver it
  • It does not weaken a program or school that earns a real surplus
  • It fits who we are: it builds on a strength, place or reputation we already have

Step 3: Build the A3, Then Rebuild It

Every idea that passes the screen gets an A3: one page, seven boxes, written by a small team. The first draft will be wrong, and that is fine. The value comes from the second and third drafts, after people whose job is to ask hard questions have had their turn.

Form the A3 team

A3 Team FILL IN
RoleJobName
OwnerHolds the pen, writes every version and decides what changes.
Numbers personFrom the CFO's office. Makes sure every figure matches the CFO's.
Voice of the students we wantSomeone who ran Phase 2 interviews. Brings the demand evidence.
BuilderA faculty or staff member who would deliver it. Says what it takes to run.

Assign the hard-question teams

Two or three people from outside the A3 team take each role below and review every draft of the A3s assigned to them. Rotate assignments so no group only reviews the work of friends. Their job is to ask, and the owner's job is to answer in the next draft.

Demand

Who outside this campus said they want this? How many? What did they choose instead? What would they pay?

Money

Do these numbers match the CFO's? What happens if enrollment comes in at half the target? What does it cost to stop?

Identity

Does this build on something true about us? Would our best graduates still recognize the college?

What we lose

What does this draw people, money or space from? Does it weaken anything that earns a surplus? How do current students finish?

Delivery

Who teaches it, where, starting when? What has to be true about accreditation, hiring and space?

Run the loop

  1. Draft 1 (one week). Fill in boxes 1 to 4 only: background, current state, target and root cause. No solutions yet. Teams that jump to the answer skip the reason students are not coming.
  2. Review 1 (one hour). Each hard-question team asks its questions. The owner writes every question in the question log and does not argue in the room.
  3. Draft 2. Rewrite boxes 1 to 4 using what you learned. Add box 5 with at least three countermeasures.
  4. Review 2. Same teams. Did the answers hold up? What new questions did the rewrite raise?
  5. Draft 3. Add the test plan and follow-up. The CFO prices it.
  6. Review 3. The red team reads it. The A3 team decides to send it to the board, rework it once more, or stop.
How to tell the loop is working

Each version should change in ways the team did not predict. If an A3 looks the same after review as it did before, nobody really reviewed it. A stopped A3 counts as a success: it saved the college time and money it did not have.

Phase 4: Decide, Pilot and Rebuild Once

By day 365, the college has chosen a direction, proven demand with real deposits and made its structural changes in a single funded round.

Step 1: The Board Decides (Day 120)

  • Board reviews the three alternatives, red team report and independent advisor's view
  • Board picks one alternative and records why
  • Board funds the transition, including a reserve for the fallback
  • Board names who is responsible for each part of execution
  • Board confirms the stopping rules still hold

Step 2: Pilot with Real Deposits

A survey says what people would like. A deposit says what they will do.

Step 3: Restructure Once

Make the foreseeable structural changes together, fund them fully and announce them clearly. Repeated small rounds of cuts keep everyone uncertain, hurt recruiting and use up trust.

  • Which students the college will serve
  • Which programs, schools and services stay, change or end
  • Which costs actually disappear (cash the college stops spending)
  • What the college will invest in, and how much
  • How current students will finish, program by program
  • How affected employees will be treated
  • Contract, accreditation and regulatory steps
  • Cost of running old and new programs side by side
  • Space and administration the smaller college will need

CABINET BOARD FACULTY

Market Examples: What a Market Read Looks Like

Six identities a college could choose to own, each with a real market read: who wants it, how big the demand is and which way it is moving, who already does it well, a warning, and the hard questions to ask. They are here to show the kind of thinking each college has to do for itself.

Read this first

None of these is your answer. A college that copies another college's identity is guessing. Yours has to come from what is already true about you (your place, your people, your history, what students say you do better than anyone nearby) and from the evidence you gather in Phase 2. Use these examples to practice the questions, then answer them for your own college.

The anatomy of a market read

  1. The claim. One sentence a family would repeat: what we are best at, and for whom.
  2. Who wants it. Students, employers and funders, with numbers and their direction of travel.
  3. Who is doing it. The colleges that already own this space, and what they did to earn it.
  4. A warning. Someone who tried and failed, and why.
  5. What it takes to win. The assets, partners and price it requires.
  6. Hard questions. The ones the A3 team and its hard-question teams should press on.
EXAMPLE 1

Faith and mission

The claim: The college most fully shaped by the values of Christ, for families who want faith at the center of education.

Who wants it

  • Catholic schools enrolled 1,683,506 students in 2024-25, a large pipeline of families who already pay for faith-based education. (NCEA, 2025)
  • That pipeline is shrinking: the number of Catholic K-12 schools fell 27% from 2000 to 2023, and 63 closed in 2024-25 while 24 opened. (Higher Ed Dive, 2025)
  • The sector is splitting. Colleges with a strong, explicit Catholic identity posted record enrollment in fall 2024, while 21 Catholic colleges announced closure or merger from 2016 to 2024. (EWTN News, 2024; OSV News, 2025)

Who is doing it

  • Franciscan University of Steubenville (Ohio) reported its tenth straight year of record enrollment in fall 2024, with 3,977 students, in the same region where other Catholic colleges are closing. (EWTN News, 2024)
  • Benedictine College (Kansas) grew to 2,247 full-time undergraduates in 2025 and recruits most students from out of state, from 48 states. (Benedictine College)

A warning

  • Catholic affiliation alone did not save Cabrini University (Pennsylvania, closed 2024) or Notre Dame College (Ohio, closed 2024). Both cited years of deficits and falling enrollment. (PhillyVoice; EWTN News)

What it takes to win

  • An identity that runs through the whole student experience, beyond a line in the mission statement.
  • National recruiting, so the college does not depend on a local school pipeline that is closing schools every year.
  • Willingness to stay small: several of the growing colleges cap enrollment near 550.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • Are we willing to become more explicit about faith, knowing some current faculty, students and donors may leave? Or are we a Catholic-heritage college competing on price?
  • Can we recruit from 40 states, or only from nearby schools?
  • Why would a family choose us over the colleges that already own this niche nationally?
EXAMPLE 2

Career and the new economy

The claim: The college where every student graduates with real work experience and a job that uses the degree.

Who wants it

  • Business is the largest bachelor's field: 375,400 degrees in 2021-22, 19% of all bachelor's degrees. (NCES Fast Facts)
  • BLS projects about 886,600 openings a year in business and financial occupations through 2035, with a median wage of $82,660 against $50,980 for all occupations. (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook)
  • Employers name internship experience among the most influential factors when choosing between equal candidates. (NACE Job Outlook 2026)

Who is doing it

  • Endicott College (Massachusetts) requires three internships of every student. It reports 99% of the Class of 2024 employed, in graduate school or in service, with 89% of those employed in work related to their major (college survey). (Endicott College)
  • Bentley University reports 98% of the Class of 2025 employed or in graduate school within six months, with a median starting salary of $75,000. (Bentley University, 2026)

A warning

  • This is the most crowded market in higher education. Southern New Hampshire University enrolled 189,531 students in fall 2024, almost all online, and awarded 8,012 business bachelor's degrees in one year. A small college selling only a business degree competes on price with that scale. (NCES College Navigator)

What it takes to win

  • Required, structured experience every year, built into the degree instead of offered as an elective.
  • Enough employers nearby to take interns every term.
  • Outcome numbers a skeptic would accept, including how many graduates responded.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • Can we name the employers who will take every one of our students each year?
  • What would a student get here that a large online university cannot give them for less?
  • Will we publish outcomes with a response rate?
EXAMPLE 3

Public service

The claim: The college that prepares people to serve: teachers, social workers, nonprofit and community leaders.

Who wants it

  • At least 411,549 teaching positions, about 1 in 8, were vacant or filled by teachers not fully certified in 2025. (Learning Policy Institute, 2025)
  • BLS projects about 293,400 openings a year in community and social service occupations through 2035, growing much faster than average. (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook)
  • Nonprofits employed 12.8 million people in 2022, about 10% of private-sector jobs. (BLS Monthly Labor Review, 2025)

Who is doing it

  • Berea College (Kentucky) asks no admitted student to pay tuition out of pocket. Every student works on campus, and the endowment covers most of the $54,700 cost per student. (Berea College catalog)
  • Nine federally recognized work colleges, including Berea, Warren Wilson and Paul Quinn, build work and service into every residential student's week. (U.S. News via WTOP, 2026)

A warning

  • Pay limits what students can borrow. Social workers earn a median $61,780 and elementary teachers $63,820. Federal loan forgiveness for public service is changing, with a new rule effective July 2026, so a plan should not depend on it. (BLS; U.S. Department of Education, 2025)

What it takes to win

  • A low net price, through endowment, a work model or partners who share the cost.
  • Signed pipelines: school districts and agencies that place and hire graduates.
  • Service built into the degree through field placements and work, more than a club.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • At our net price, can a teacher or social worker repay what they borrow here without loan forgiveness?
  • Which districts and agencies will sign placement and hiring agreements, and will any help pay?
  • Without Berea's endowment, what keeps our price low enough?
EXAMPLE 4

Environment and the outdoors

The claim: The college for people who want to work on conservation, climate, energy and the land.

Who wants it

  • BLS projects environmental scientists at 93,400 jobs, growing 6% through 2035, with about 7,300 openings a year and a median wage of $82,220. (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook)
  • Solar installers and wind turbine technicians remain among the fastest-growing occupations, though BLS lowered both projections in its newest table, and the numbers of jobs are small. (BLS Employment Projections)
  • US demand for workers with green skills grew 9.8% while the supply grew 3.1%. (LinkedIn Green Skills Report 2024, via Yale)

Who is doing it

  • College of the Atlantic (Maine) has about 350 students, no departments and one degree, human ecology, with a required internship and capstone. Small, distinctive and coherent. (College of the Atlantic)
  • Unity Environmental University (Maine) grew from about 600 students to more than 7,500 by 2023, almost entirely through distance education. Its residential program closed. (Unity Environmental University)

A warning

  • Green Mountain College (Vermont) built its brand on sustainability and closed in 2019, after undergraduate enrollment fell from 709 to 468. The mission fit; the economics of a small residential campus in a shrinking region did not. The 2025 federal tax law also cut clean energy credits, which makes that part of the job outlook less certain. (Seven Days, 2019; FTI Consulting, 2025)

What it takes to win

  • Place-based assets students cannot get elsewhere: land, water, farms, field stations.
  • Hiring partners beyond advocacy groups: agencies, utilities, land trusts and consultancies.
  • A delivery model whose costs match demand.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • Which bachelor's-level jobs will our graduates fill, and who hires them in our region?
  • Does our plan still hold if federal clean energy money shrinks further?
  • What do we offer that College of the Atlantic, Unity's online programs and the state flagship do not?
EXAMPLE 5

Government and policy

The claim: The college that turns its location in a state capital or near Washington into careers in public life.

Who wants it

  • State and local governments employed 20.3 million people in March 2025, up 1.4% in a year. (Census Bureau, 2025)
  • 46% of state and local HR leaders say their biggest retirement wave is still ahead, and 61% have no succession plan. (MissionSquare Research Institute, 2025)
  • Federal employment is shrinking: about 317,000 people left federal jobs in 2025 and 68,000 were hired. Universities near Washington have reported enrollment effects. (OPM via FEDmanager, 2026)

Who is doing it

  • Willamette University sits across the street from Oregon's capitol and leads with capitol internships. Full-time undergraduates rose from 1,637 to 1,715 in fall 2025, though the growth cannot be tied to the policy identity alone. (Willamette Collegian, 2025)
  • Patrick Henry College (Virginia) built a government-focused college of 436 students with internships across federal agencies and Congress, and reports all of its 2024 graduates employed or in graduate school (college survey). (Patrick Henry College)

A warning

  • Location is not an identity. Millsaps College, in Mississippi's capital, fell from 850 students in 2019 to 637 undergraduates in 2022. (Clarion Ledger, 2024)

What it takes to win

  • A placement pipeline families can see: named offices, agencies and employers, every year.
  • A focus on state and local roles and their retirement wave, more than federal jobs alone.
  • Published outcomes.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • Is our plan tied to federal hiring that is shrinking, or to state and local hiring that is growing?
  • Which offices will take our students every year, in writing?
  • Why would a student pay our price instead of interning at the capitol from the public university nearby?
EXAMPLE 6

Working with AI

The claim: The college whose graduates know how to do their field's work well alongside AI.

Who wants it

  • Job postings that ask for AI skills pay 28% more, about $18,000 a year, and 51% of them in 2024 were outside IT and computer science. (Lightcast, 2025)
  • 86% of students use AI in their studies, and 58% say they lack enough AI knowledge and skill. (Digital Education Council, 2024)
  • Employers are dropping degree requirements for AI-augmented jobs, from 66% to 59% between 2019 and 2024, which raises the bar for showing that a degree pays. (PwC AI Jobs Barometer, 2025)

Who is doing it

  • Babson College tied AI to its existing identity in entrepreneurship: an interdisciplinary AI lab, peer training for faculty and AI in the first-year management course. (Babson College, 2024)
  • Connecticut College funded a three-year AI program for faculty with a bequest and plans to share its materials with other small liberal arts colleges. (Connecticut College, 2025)

A warning

  • Everyone is claiming this. Ohio State promised AI fluency for every graduate starting with the Class of 2029, and Purdue made AI competency a graduation requirement. AI readiness alone will soon be expected everywhere and will not set a college apart. (Ohio State, 2025)

What it takes to win

  • AI applied inside a field the college already owns, such as nursing, accounting, teaching or design.
  • Every faculty member trained and paid for it, beyond a few volunteers.
  • Proof: graduate portfolios and employers who hire for AI-augmented roles.

Hard questions for the A3 team

  • Large public universities promise AI fluency at public prices. What does our version give that theirs cannot?
  • Can our faculty teach this credibly within 18 months, and what does that cost?
  • Which of our existing strengths does AI make more valuable?

More on AI inside the college: where it helps, what faculty worry about, and a technology audit (from Jeff Ritter's original appendix)

Where software helps, and where people matter most

Duolingo teaches more than 100 million people a month with almost no human instructors. For most learners it is a vocabulary drill. It handles the structured, repetitive part of learning well and leaves the rest to people: holding a real conversation, reading a poem, finding your way in another culture. AI tools behave the same way on campus. They are good at sorting, drafting, answering routine questions and giving practice. Judgment, meaning, relationships and surprise belong to faculty and staff.

What faculty worry about, and how to respond

  1. Say where the tools stop. AI can handle drill, practice and routine feedback. Teaching, judging and mentoring stay with faculty.
  2. Start with tools that help faculty. A tool that sorts student responses before class makes the professor more effective. A tool that grades essays makes the professor feel watched. Start with the first kind.
  3. Let faculty choose their pace. Begin with volunteers. Others will follow when they see it work for colleagues.
  4. Protect academic judgment. The professor sets the grade, designs the assignment and leads the class.
  5. Show the math. If better advising tools free 200 staff hours a month, show faculty where those hours went.

A3 Quick Reference

The workflow lives in Phase 3, Step 3. Two kinds of A3 come up during the year:

Problem A3

Question: Why is this measure falling short, and how do we fix it?

Time frame: Weeks to months

Example: First-year retention fell from 78 to 70 percent

Use when: The offering is right and something about delivery has slipped

Strategy A3

Question: Where should we go, and how will we prove it works?

Time frame: One to five years

Example: An evening degree for working adults in our region

Use when: The question is whether the offering itself is right

Common mistake

Treating a fit problem as a delivery problem. If several problem A3s point back to the same answer, that people do not want this enough at this price, stop fixing and write a strategy A3.

Words to Know

A3

A one-page format with seven boxes. A small team writes it, hears hard questions and rewrites it, several times.

Contribution

What a program brings in minus the costs that go away without it.

Discount Rate

College-funded aid as a share of sticker tuition.

Hard-Question Team

Two or three people outside an A3 team who review each draft from one angle, such as demand or money.

Red Team

A small group whose job is to show how a plan could fail.

TAM, SAM, SOM

Everyone who could want an offering, the part we can serve, and the share we can realistically win.

Runway

Months of spendable cash at the current rate of loss.

Stopping Rule

A line set in advance that triggers a set action when crossed.

Teach-out

A plan that lets current students finish their degree when a program ends.
