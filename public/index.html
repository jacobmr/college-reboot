Here We Are

Our college spends more each year than it brings in, and doing a little less of the same thing will not fix that. This section explains why in plain terms, and why the answer depends on all of us.

Fewer students, more choices

The number of Americans finishing high school peaked in 2025 at roughly 3.8 to 3.9 million. It is projected to fall about 13 percent by 2041, and 38 states will have fewer graduates in 2041 than in 2023. The Northeast and Midwest have already started to decline.

Fewer students means every college competes harder for each one. The most common response is a bigger scholarship. Private colleges now give back an average of about 57 percent of sticker tuition to first-year students as their own aid, a record. Each point of discount is money the college never collects.

The core question

Even after the decline, more than three million people will finish high school every year. Millions of adults want a degree or a new skill. They will all go somewhere. How do we make sure it is here?

What the market wants decides what we can sell

Think of a concert hall with empty seats. If people do not want to hear the program, a bigger poster will not fill the hall. Better marketing tells more people what we offer. It cannot make them want it.

Business people call this product/market fit: offering something enough people want, at a price they will pay, that costs less to deliver than it brings in. When a college has a fit problem, the offering has to change: what we teach, how, when, where, for whom and at what price.

How big is the market? TAM, SAM and SOM

Before anyone argues for an idea, we should know how many people might want it. Investors size a market in three steps, and the same steps work for a college.

TermWhat it means for a college
TAM: Total Addressable MarketEveryone who could want this kind of education and whom we could plausibly reach. For example, adults in our region who started college and never finished.
SAM: Serviceable Available MarketThe part of the TAM we can actually serve: close enough to campus or within our online reach, interested in fields we teach, ready for our academic level and able to pay our net price.
SOM: Serviceable Obtainable MarketThe share of the SAM we can realistically win each year within three years, after the community college, the state university and the big online programs take their share.

The TAM always looks large and means little. The SOM is the number that pays salaries. Be wary of any SOM above a few percent of the SAM unless the interviews in Phase 2 back it up, because every competitor is reading the same demographic charts we are.

What the example teaches

A good idea with real demand closed about a tenth of the gap. Most colleges will need two or three ideas, a smaller cost base, or both. The board should expect that answer and plan for it from the start.

Every college needs its own answer

There is no template for a rescued college. What works for a nursing college in a small city will not work for an arts college in the mountains or a faith-based college in the suburbs. The plan that saves us will build on what is already true about us: our place, our people, our reputation and the things students say we do better than anyone nearby.

The tools in this workbook are the same for every college. The answer will be ours, and it will come from the evidence we gather in Phase 2 and from an honest look at who we are.

Why the people closest to the college should not set the direction

Current students chose us. Faculty built what we offer. Both are the people for whom the current college already works. That makes them the right people to tell us what must not be lost, and the wrong people to tell us what the students who are not choosing us want.

This workbook asks faculty and students to do real work: supply facts, propose ideas, test them, design the new offering and deliver it. The direction itself comes from evidence about the students we are trying to reach.

Why we are sharing more than usual

Colleges in trouble often keep the numbers quiet, hope next year's class is bigger and protect the identity they have. Each of those feels prudent. Together they use up the time and money needed to change.

Plans also fail when leaders decide in a small room, agree too quickly and avoid being wrong in public. The cure is more people looking at the same facts, and someone whose job is to say the plan will not work.

The Six Ideas That Explain Every College Budget

You do not need a business degree to use them, and you do need all six to judge any idea for the college's future.

1. Sticker Price & Net Price

The sticker price is the published tuition. Almost no one pays it. The net price is what a student actually pays after the college's own scholarships and grants.

2. Discount Rate

The share of sticker tuition the college gives back as its own aid. If tuition is $50,000 and the average student receives $27,500 in college-funded aid, the discount rate is 55%.

3. Net Tuition Revenue

Students times net price. This is the money that pays salaries. A college can enroll more students and still collect less money if it discounts more to get them.

4. Cost Per Student

Total operating spending divided by students. Most of that cost is fixed in the short run: buildings, debt, most salaries.

5. Contribution

What one more student or program brings in minus the costs that go away if it goes away. A program with negative contribution makes the gap bigger every year it runs.

6. Cash Runway

How many months the college can pay its bills from cash and investments it is allowed to spend, at its current rate of loss. Runway is the clock.

Key lesson

Discounting more to fill seats can make the gap worse. Raise the discount rate from 55% to 60% and each student brings in $2,500 less. Across 1,500 students that is $3.75 million a year.

Three Rules for the Whole Year

  1. Numbers first, opinions second. Every idea gets tested against the same worksheets.
  2. The people who are here help build the future, and the people who are not here tell us what it must be. Prospective students set the direction.
  3. Change moves at the speed of trust. Share more than feels comfortable, on a schedule, and never share a number you will have to take back.

Phase 1: Face the Numbers

By day 30, the president and CFO produce one page that states the size of the gap, the months of runway and the enrollment the college can expect.

Gather First

  • Five to ten years of audited financial statements
  • Five to ten years of IRS Form 990 filings
  • Five to ten years of fall enrollment, entering class, yield and retention (IPEDS reports)
  • Each year's original budget beside actual results
  • Current debt schedule and loan covenants
  • Revenue and direct cost by program and by school

Red Team Checklist

  • Three to five people, named by the board
  • At least one with finance expertise from outside the college
  • At least one from outside higher education
  • No one who wrote the plan under review
  • Access to the same data as the cabinet
Habits that keep a small room honest

The president speaks last in every decision meeting. Run a premortem: before approving a plan, everyone writes privately why it failed two years from now. Write forecasts down with dates and authors. Reward the bad news.

Interview Guide

  1. Tell me how you decided where to go. What were the finalists?
  2. What did the place you chose offer that the others did not?
  3. What did you think of us? Where did that impression come from?
  4. What did it cost you in the end, after aid? How much did price matter?
  5. What would have to be true for you to choose a college like ours?
  6. If we offered [describe an idea in one sentence], would you have considered it?
  7. What do you want to be able to do in five years that college should help with?

Phase 3: Design and Test Ideas

By day 120, working teams turn the strongest ideas into one-page A3 proposals. Each A3 goes through at least three rounds of hard questions, gets priced by the CFO and faces the red team before the board sees it. Anyone can submit an idea.

Step 1: The Idea Card

One idea per card. Keep it to one page.

Step 2: The Screen

An idea moves to an A3 team only if the answer to all six is yes.

  • We have evidence of demand from people who are not current students
  • At full size it closes a meaningful percent of the annual gap within three years
  • We can test it with real applications and deposits within twelve months
  • We have, or can affordably get, the people and space to deliver it
  • It does not weaken a program or school that earns a real surplus
  • It fits who we are: it builds on a strength, place or reputation we already have

Step 3: Build the A3, Then Rebuild It

Every idea that passes the screen gets an A3: one page, seven boxes, written by a small team. The first draft will be wrong, and that is fine. The value comes from the second and third drafts, after people whose job is to ask hard questions have had their turn.

Form the A3 team

A3 Team FILL IN
RoleJobName
OwnerHolds the pen, writes every version and decides what changes.
Numbers personFrom the CFO's office. Makes sure every figure matches the CFO's.
Voice of the students we wantSomeone who ran Phase 2 interviews. Brings the demand evidence.
BuilderA faculty or staff member who would deliver it. Says what it takes to run.

Assign the hard-question teams

Two or three people from outside the A3 team take each role below and review every draft of the A3s assigned to them. Rotate assignments so no group only reviews the work of friends. Their job is to ask, and the owner's job is to answer in the next draft.

Demand

Who outside this campus said they want this? How many? What did they choose instead? What would they pay?

Money

Do these numbers match the CFO's? What happens if enrollment comes in at half the target? What does it cost to stop?

Identity

Does this build on something true about us? Would our best graduates still recognize the college?

What we lose

What does this draw people, money or space from? Does it weaken anything that earns a surplus? How do current students finish?

Delivery

Who teaches it, where, starting when? What has to be true about accreditation, hiring and space?

Run the loop

  1. Draft 1 (one week). Fill in boxes 1 to 4 only: background, current state, target and root cause. No solutions yet. Teams that jump to the answer skip the reason students are not coming.
  2. Review 1 (one hour). Each hard-question team asks its questions. The owner writes every question in the question log and does not argue in the room.
  3. Draft 2. Rewrite boxes 1 to 4 using what you learned. Add box 5 with at least three countermeasures.
  4. Review 2. Same teams. Did the answers hold up? What new questions did the rewrite raise?
  5. Draft 3. Add the test plan and follow-up. The CFO prices it.
  6. Review 3. The red team reads it. The A3 team decides to send it to the board, rework it once more, or stop.
How to tell the loop is working

Each version should change in ways the team did not predict. If an A3 looks the same after review as it did before, nobody really reviewed it. A stopped A3 counts as a success: it saved the college time and money it did not have.

Phase 4: Decide, Pilot and Rebuild Once

By day 365, the college has chosen a direction, proven demand with real deposits and made its structural changes in a single funded round.

Step 1: The Board Decides (Day 120)

  • Board reviews the three alternatives, red team report and independent advisor's view
  • Board picks one alternative and records why
  • Board funds the transition, including a reserve for the fallback
  • Board names who is responsible for each part of execution
  • Board confirms the stopping rules still hold

Step 2: Pilot with Real Deposits

A survey says what people would like. A deposit says what they will do.

Step 3: Restructure Once

Make the foreseeable structural changes together, fund them fully and announce them clearly. Repeated small rounds of cuts keep everyone uncertain, hurt recruiting and use up trust.

  • Which students the college will serve
  • Which programs, schools and services stay, change or end
  • Which costs actually disappear (cash the college stops spending)
  • What the college will invest in, and how much
  • How current students will finish, program by program
  • How affected employees will be treated
  • Contract, accreditation and regulatory steps
  • Cost of running old and new programs side by side
  • Space and administration the smaller college will need

OPTIONAL APPENDIX — by Jeff Ritter

Appendix C: The Technology Layer

This appendix is a companion to the core workbook. A college that restructures its programs and governance but runs them on the same technology has solved half the problem. This section covers the other half — how AI and integrated systems can change the cost structure of the rebuilt college.

The Duolingo Ceiling

Duolingo teaches 100 million people a month with almost no human instructors. It is also, for most learners, a vocabulary drill. It works brilliantly for the structured, repetitive part of learning. It fails at the rest: holding a real conversation, understanding a poem, navigating a culture.

No amount of AI improvement fixes this, because the limitation is not the technology. It is the nature of the work.

AI handles the individual, repetitive, structured parts of learning very well and at very low cost. It does not handle judgment, meaning, relationship or surprise.

The principle

AI prepares the ground. Humans lead the learning that matters.

Practical example: An AI tool clusters student responses to a reading assignment before class. The professor sees that 40% misunderstood the central argument, 30% understood it but cannot apply it, and 30% are ready to move on. The professor walks in and teaches three different groups in one session. Without the tool, the professor lectures to the middle and hopes for the best. The AI did not teach. It made the teaching better.

What Faculty Fear and What to Do About It

  1. Name the ceiling. AI handles drill, practice and routine feedback. It does not handle what you do. We are investing in AI so that more of your time goes to the work only you can do.
  2. Start with tools that help faculty. An AI that clusters student responses before class makes the professor more effective. An AI that grades essays makes the professor feel watched. Start with the first kind.
  3. Let faculty choose their pace. The pilot should have early adopters who want to experiment, not a mandate. Adoption spreads through proof, not policy.
  4. Protect academic judgment. The professor decides the grade, designs the assignment, leads the class. AI prepares, sorts, drafts, and flags. The human decides.
  5. Show the math. If AI-assisted advising frees 200 staff hours a month, show faculty that those hours went to student support, not to a budget line that disappeared.

The one sentence to remember

Technology does not save a college. A better offering for students who will pay for it saves a college. Technology makes that offering cheaper to deliver, better for the student, and possible to run with the smaller staff and budget the restructured college will have.

Appendices

A3 Quick Reference

The workflow lives in Phase 3, Step 3. Two kinds of A3 come up during the year:

Problem A3

Question: Why is this measure falling short, and how do we fix it?

Time frame: Weeks to months

Example: First-year retention fell from 78 to 70 percent

Use when: The offering is right and something about delivery has slipped

Strategy A3

Question: Where should we go, and how will we prove it works?

Time frame: One to five years

Example: An evening degree for working adults in our region

Use when: The question is whether the offering itself is right

Common mistake

Treating a fit problem as a delivery problem. If several problem A3s point back to the same answer, that people do not want this enough at this price, stop fixing and write a strategy A3.
